Introduction

The previous note in the series 6 showed that, in the disruption of three-body systems with unequal masses, the rule "the pair of the two heaviest remains" predicts the outcome better than any energy signal from the trajectories. This leaves open the question of the role of velocity. In the theory of encounters between binaries and single bodies, the outcome depends on the comparison between the orbital velocity of the binary and the arrival velocity of the intruder: hard binaries, which orbit rapidly relative to a slow intruder, tend to tighten and to fling it out, and soft binaries tend to be disrupted 1,2.

This note tests whether this velocity comparison predicts which body will be ejected and, above all, whether it adds information to the masses. The result is that velocity carries a weak signal, useful only when the masses do not decide.

Method

Simulations

We use the same regularized integrator 4,5, the same ejection criteria, and the same two ensembles of initial conditions as in the previous note 6: start from rest and virial ratio 0.5, in planar simulations with \(G = 1\). We performed 300 runs per ensemble for the mass groups (1, 1, 1), (1, 1, 3), (1, 1, 10), (3, 3, 1), and (10, 10, 1), with the same seeds as in the previous note, and 600 runs per ensemble with independently drawn masses, log-uniform between 1 and 10, for a total of 4,200 runs. We analyzed the runs with an ejection preceded by at least one pericenter and a relative energy error below \(10^{-4}\), between 245 and 597 per row.

Velocity ratio

For each candidate body \(k\), we consider the pair formed by the other two, \(i\) and \(j\), and define

\[ \rho_k = \frac{v_{\rm orb}}{V_k}, \qquad v_{\rm orb}^2 = \frac{2\,|E_{ij}|}{\mu_{ij}}, \]

where \(E_{ij} < 0\) is the energy of the pair, \(\mu_{ij}\) its reduced mass, and \(V_k\) the velocity of \(k\) relative to the center of mass of the pair; if the pair is not bound, \(\rho_k = 0\). Large values of \(\rho_k\) correspond to a pair that orbits rapidly relative to a slow third body, the case in which the ejection of \(k\) is expected.

Predictors

We compare, at fractions \(f = 0.25\), 0.5, 0.75, and 0.9 of the time to ejection: (i) masses: the lightest body is ejected, with ties counted as a fractional success; (ii) instantaneous velocity: the \(k\) with the largest \(\rho_k\) at the instant \(f\,t_{\rm ej}\) is ejected; (iii) mean velocity: the \(k\) with the largest time average of \(\ln \rho_k\) over the interval \([0, f\,t_{\rm ej}]\) is ejected, with \(\rho_k\) bounded from below by 0.01; (iv) combined model: a conditional logit, in which the probability of ejection of \(k\) is proportional to \(\exp(\beta_1 \ln m_k + \beta_2 \ln \rho_k + \beta_3 \langle \ln \rho_k \rangle)\), fitted separately in each ensemble, with all mass groups, and evaluated by fivefold cross-validation.

Three patterns stand out. With equal masses, the velocity ratio carries a weak signal: from 34% to 47% success, against 33% for chance, stronger in the virial ensemble. With unequal masses and one clearly lightest body, velocity alone loses to the masses by a wide margin, for example 60% to 70% against 91% to 94% in the (3, 3, 1) group. And the combined model only outperforms the mass predictor when the masses are tied, that is, when there are two equal light bodies: in the (1, 1, 3) and (1, 1, 10) groups, the success rate rises from about 50% to 54% to 66%, because velocity then breaks the tie between the two candidates. With random masses, the most general case, the combined model does not improve on the mass predictor: 72% and 76%, against 71% and 76% for the masses alone.

The fitted coefficients confirm this reading. In both ensembles, \(\beta_1 \approx -2.4\), whereas \(\beta_2\) and \(\beta_3\) lie between 0.02 and 0.11. The model therefore assigns an ejection probability approximately proportional to \(m^{-2.4}\), close to the approximate \(m^{-3}\) law 3, and almost no weight to velocity.

Discussion

The absence of gain with unequal masses has a simple explanation. The ratio \(\rho_k\) already depends on the masses: with one light body, the pair formed by the two heavy ones tends to be the most bound and the fastest, and the arrival velocity of the light body reflects the same imbalance. The velocity information is largely redundant with the mass information. When the masses are tied, this redundancy disappears, and what remains of the velocity is useful to break the tie.

The weak signal in the equal-mass case is consistent with the result of the previous note 6: in the chaotic regime, the configuration changes at each encounter, and a measurement taken before the last close encounter says little about it. The theory of encounters with hard and soft binaries 1,2 describes well the mean outcome of an isolated encounter, but, in a chaotic sequence of encounters, each one erases part of the information from the previous one.

The main limitations are the following. The simulations are planar, and the start from rest has zero total angular momentum. The ratio \(\rho_k\) is one choice among several possible ones; measures restricted to the closest encounters, or that take into account the geometry of the encounter, could carry more information. The combined model is linear in the logarithms and does not explore interactions between the variables. With 245 to 597 runs per row, the binomial error is two to three percentage points.

Conclusions

The comparison between the orbital velocity of the pair and the arrival velocity of the third body does not replace the masses as a predictor of the ejected body and adds almost no information to them. It works as a tiebreaker when two candidates have the same mass and gives a weak signal when the three masses are equal. As a by-product, the fit independently recovers an ejection law close to \(m^{-3}\).
