Introduction

In Kol's flux theory, the probability of each outcome of a three-body system is the product of a factor known in closed form, the phase-volume flux, and an absorptivity, which to this day must be measured by simulation 1,3. Note FR-TC-10 showed that the absorptivity of a whole run is fixed in the first passage of the incoming body through the pair: it is the chance that this passage ends in chaos or leaves the outgoing body bound 5. Note FR-TC-11 showed that, in this passage, the choice between flyby and partner exchange follows a simple mass law 6. To arrive at a formula for the absorptivity, the second half is missing: given who leaves, the chance that it stays bound to the pair instead of escaping. This note measures that chance, proposes approximate laws for it, including the effect of the eccentricity of the pair, and tests the resulting formula without simulating the cases to which it is applied.

Method

We use the 42,415 regular first passages (with at most one scramble) from the grids of note FR-TC-10 (eight mass channels) and note FR-TC-11 (28 mass combinations), in planar motion with zero total angular momentum, with three values of eccentricity (0.15, 0.5, and 0.85) and relative energies of the pair \(x = E_B/E_0\) from 1.05 to 10 5,6. The outgoing body stays bound when the energy of the pair after the passage, \(x_{\rm novo}\), is below the escape threshold, \(x_{\rm novo} < 1\). We separate the two branches: the flyby, in which the incoming body leaves, and the exchange, in which one of the binary members leaves. For each branch, we fit logistic models of the chance of staying bound and measure the predictive ability by leaving out, each time, one mass combination.

For the final test, we fit all the constants only with the mass grid of note FR-TC-11 and apply the formula to the seven channels of notes FR-TC-09 and FR-TC-10, whose mass combinations are not in that grid. We compare the absorptivity of the formula with the measured one in 924 energy and eccentricity cells 4,5 and, multiplying it by the flux, we predict the escape chances and the distribution of the final eccentricity in the free falls of these sets.

Results

What happens to the pair in each branch

Let \(D = \ln(x_{\rm novo}/x)\) be the logarithmic change in the energy of the pair in the first passage. In the flyby, the pair leaves on average less bound than it entered (median \(D\) of \(-0.14\)), and the effect grows with the binding of the pair. In the exchange, \(D\) depends mainly on the masses: if the new pair is heavier than the old one, it becomes more bound, and the released energy pushes the expelled body away. The simplest estimate, in which the new pair keeps the semimajor axis of the old one, gives \(D_0 = \ln(m_1 m_2 / m_a m_b)\), where \(m_1\) and \(m_2\) are the masses of the new pair; the measured median of \(D\) correlates with \(D_0\) with \(r = 0.77\). In the exchange between bodies of the same mass, the Newton's cradle effect of note FR-TC-11, \(D_0 = 0\), and the pair hardly changes.

Laws for the chance of staying bound

Fitted only with the mass grid, the most compact laws are

\[ P_{\rm bound}^{\rm fly} = \sigma\!\left(-0.24 - 1.62\,\ln x + 0.54\,\ln\frac{m_s}{m_a + m_b}\right), \]
\[ P_{\rm bound}^{\rm exch} = \sigma\!\left(-0.08 - 1.56\,\ln x - 1.06\,D_0\right), \]

where \(\sigma(z) = 1/(1 + e^{-z})\) and \(m_s\) is the mass of the incoming body. Table I summarizes the predictive ability. The exchange law explains 72% of the variation among cells in a mass combination not used in the fit; the flyby law, 41% to 48%. The eccentricity hardly appears on average, but its effect is specific to each combination: in a heavy pair in a nearly circular orbit, a light body that flies by never stays bound, while, with the pair in an eccentric orbit, it sometimes does. The quantity that best captures this effect is the ratio between the flyby speed and the orbital speed of the pair at apoapsis, where the pair spends most of its time. With it, the flyby law rises to 54%, and the flyby or exchange law, evaluated in the same way, rises from 78% to 88%.

The complete formula

Combining the two parts, the absorptivity of an arrival with relative energy \(x\) is

\[ \mathcal{A} = P_{\rm exch}\,\bar P_{\rm bound}^{\rm exch} + \left(1 - P_{\rm exch}\right) P_{\rm bound}^{\rm fly}, \]
\[ P_{\rm exch} = \frac{1}{1 + (0.52\,m_{\max}/m_s)^{1.9}}, \]

with eight constants, all fitted on the mass grid. Here \(\bar P_{\rm bound}^{\rm exch}\) is the mean of \(P_{\rm bound}^{\rm exch}\) between the two possible cases, in which \(m_a\) leaves or \(m_b\) leaves, which expresses the coin toss between the binary members observed in note FR-TC-11, and \(m_{\max}\) is the heavier member. The version with eccentricity uses the same three parts, with fifteen constants. The formula ignores direct chaos in the first passage, which ranges from 0% to 8% depending on the channel.

Applied to the seven channels that did not take part in the fit, the formula reproduces the measured absorptivity with \(R^2 = 0.72\) to \(0.77\) when the average over eccentricity is compared, and with \(R^2 = 0.59\) and a mean error of 0.10 cell by cell (Fig. 1). The version with eccentricity does not improve the cell-by-cell comparison: the eccentricity effect learned on the grid does not transfer well to the other channels.

Escape chances without simulating the case

Multiplying the flux by the absorptivity of the formula, we predict the escape chances in the free falls of the three sets of unequal masses (Table II). The mean error in the five fractions is 1.7 to 1.8 percentage points, against 4.2 for the volume theory 2, 2.5 for the flux without absorptivity, and 1.0 to 1.6 for the flux with the absorptivity measured by simulation 4. The largest deviation is in the heavy body of the set (3, 3, 1), which the formula overestimates, as the measured absorptivity already did.

The final eccentricity remains open

The distribution of the final eccentricity is where the volume theory errs the most and where the measured absorptivity does best 4. The formula falls in between (Table III). With equal masses, it takes the predicted mean eccentricity from 0.786, that of the pure flux, to 0.734, against 0.716 measured; the measured absorptivity gave 0.718 to 0.723. The shape of the distribution, however, still differs (Kolmogorov-Smirnov distance of 0.09, against 0.01 to 0.02 with the measured absorptivity). In the unequal sets, the formula does well for (1, 1, 3) and (1, 2, 3) and errs for (3, 3, 1).

Discussion

The piece of Kol's theory that depended on simulation can be approximated by an eight-constant formula, built from two simple physical rules: a mass threshold decides whether there is a partner exchange, and the energy of the pair, together with the change in the mass of the pair in the exchange, decides whether the expelled body stays bound. Used without any simulation of the tested cases, the formula predicts the escape chances with a mean error of about 1.7 percentage points, better than the flux without absorptivity and than the volume theory, and close to the result with the measured absorptivity. In practice, this means that the table of chances of who is expelled, for any masses in the regime studied, can be computed with an integral instead of hundreds of thousands of simulations.

What is missing is the eccentricity. Its effect on the absorptivity is strong in some channels, such as that of a light body facing a heavy, nearly circular pair, and almost null in others, and the speed of the pair at apoapsis captures only part of it. That is why the formula improves the mean eccentricity but does not reproduce the shape of the distribution. The structure of the arrival maps of note FR-TC-10 suggests the reason: with an eccentric pair, the outcome of the passage depends strongly on the orbital phase at which the body arrives, and an average over the phases hides regions with opposite behaviors 5.

The main limitations are as follows. All the laws are empirical and hold for the plane with zero total angular momentum and for the ranges of masses, energies, and eccentricities of the grid. The formula ignores direct chaos in the first passage and treats the choice of the expelled member as a coin toss, which is approximate when the incoming body is light. The final test uses three mass sets, and the deviation in the set (3, 3, 1) shows that the accuracy varies among channels.

Conclusions

The absorptivity of Kol's flux theory, in the plane with zero angular momentum, can be approximated by an eight-constant formula that combines a mass rule (flyby or exchange) and an energy rule (staying bound or escaping). Without simulating the tested cases, it reproduces the measured absorptivity with \(R^2\) of 0.72 to 0.77, on average over eccentricity, and predicts the escape chances with a mean error of 1.7 percentage points. The dependence on eccentricity still has no general law, and the distribution of the final eccentricity remains the part of the prediction that requires the measured absorptivity.
