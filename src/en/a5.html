Introduction

In the nonhierarchical regime, the three-body problem almost always ends in the same way: two bodies form a binary and the third is ejected 1,2. The identity of the ejected body and the properties of the final binary admit a statistical description 2,3, but the individual trajectory is chaotic. This note asks a more specific question: is it possible to recognize, in the trajectories, the binary that will survive before the third body is ejected? If so, how far in advance, and does this depend on the mass ratios?

We test three classes of masses: three equal masses, two small and one large, and two large and one small. We also include a scaling control, with the equal masses multiplied by ten, to verify that, in Newtonian gravity, a uniform change of mass only alters the time scale.

Method

Initial conditions and ensembles

The simulations are planar, in units with \(G = 1\). The three initial positions are drawn uniformly in the unit disk, rejecting pairs closer than 0.1, and the center of mass is moved to the origin. We use two ensembles: start from rest (cold collapse) and start with random velocities rescaled to virial ratio \(K/|W| = 0.5\). The mass groups are (1, 1, 1), (10, 10, 10), (1, 1, 3), (1, 1, 10), (3, 3, 1), and (10, 10, 1), with 500 runs per group and per ensemble. The (10, 10, 10) case was run twice: with the same seeds as the (1, 1, 1) case and with independent seeds. Time is measured in units of the crossing time \(t_{\rm cr} = G M^{5/2}/(2|E|)^{3/2}\), where \(M\) is the total mass and \(E\) the energy.

Integration and ejection criterion

The integration uses algorithmic regularization in chain coordinates 4,5, with Gragg-Bulirsch-Stoer extrapolation and tolerance \(10^{-13}\), without softening of the potential. The relative energy error had a median of about \(3 \times 10^{-13}\) and a maximum of \(2 \times 10^{-9}\); no run was discarded. A body \(k\) is considered ejected when the pair formed by the other two is bound, the energy of \(k\) relative to the center of mass of the pair is positive, \(k\) is receding, and its distance exceeds 20 times the semimajor axis of the pair. The ejection time \(t_{\rm ej}\) is that of the last pericenter of \(k\) before escape. Runs without ejection up to \(2000\,t_{\rm cr}\) are recorded as unresolved.

Measured signals

Along each trajectory we compute the pair energies

\[ E_{ij} = \tfrac{1}{2}\mu_{ij} v_{ij}^2 - \frac{G m_i m_j}{r_{ij}}, \qquad \mu_{ij} = \frac{m_i m_j}{m_i + m_j}, \]

and call the most bound pair the one with the lowest \(E_{ij}\). We define \(t_{\rm lock}\) as the last instant at which the most bound pair becomes the final pair, and the lead time as \(t_{\rm ej} - t_{\rm lock}\). We evaluate the predictor "the final pair is the currently most bound pair" at fractions \(f = 0.25\), 0.5, 0.75, and 0.9 of \(t_{\rm ej}\), and compare it with the naive predictor "the two heaviest bodies remain" and with chance (1/3). An escape attempt is an excursion of the body that will be ejected beyond five semimajor axes of the final binary, while still bound, followed by a return.

Results

Mass scaling

The (1, 1, 1) and (10, 10, 10) cases are statistically indistinguishable when time is measured in \(t_{\rm cr}\): in the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test, \(p \approx 0.9998\) for the distribution of \(t_{\rm ej}/t_{\rm cr}\) with the same seeds, and \(p = 0.94\) and \(0.67\) with independent seeds, in the two ensembles. In absolute time, the more massive case evolves about \(10^{1/2}\) times faster, as predicted by the scaling \(t \propto M^{-1/2}\). With the same seeds, the runs that end before \(10\,t_{\rm cr}\) are identical; in the longer ones, the outcome begins to diverge, because rounding differences are amplified by chaos.

Lead time and predictors

With equal masses, there is no early signal: the median lead time is practically zero, which means that the final pair only becomes definitively the most bound at the last close encounter, and the most bound pair predictor is correct 35% to 45% of the time at any fraction of \(t_{\rm ej}\), slightly above chance. Fig. 1 compares the predictors for the start from rest. The signal grows with the mass disparity and is largest in the (10, 10, 1) case, in which the final pair is already formed, in the median, for about half (rest) to four fifths (virial) of the time preceding the ejection. In all groups with unequal masses, however, the naive predictor, based only on the masses, is as good as or better than the dynamical predictor. In the (1, 1, 3) case, the naive predictor cannot distinguish the two light masses and stays near 50%.

Escape attempts and final binary

With equal masses, there are on average 10 to 12 escape attempts before the ejection. With two large masses and one small, the ejection is faster and more direct: fewer than two attempts on average for the start from rest. The difference is not explained by the shorter duration alone: the median rate of attempts per crossing time falls from 0.17 (equal masses) to 0.03 (3, 3, 1) and zero (10, 10, 1). The binding energies of the final pair increase in just over half of the transitions between consecutive attempts (54% to 76%), only a weak stepwise tightening. The final binaries are highly eccentric, with mean eccentricity between 0.68 and 0.99.

Discussion

Conservation of momentum explains the asymmetry. In a close encounter, the bodies exchange equal amounts of momentum, and for the small mass this represents a large change in velocity. With two large masses, a single encounter is usually enough to throw the small one out, and the identity of the final pair is decided early. With equal masses, no body has this disadvantage, and the system exchanges partners several times before the outcome, so that the information about the final binary only appears at the last encounter. This is consistent with Heggie's law 6: encounters with hard binaries tend to tighten them and to fling out the intruder.

The finding that the masses predict better than any energy signal indicates that, in the chaotic regime, the information useful for the outcome lies more in the global conditions (masses and energy) than in the details of the trajectory, which is the premise of the statistical solutions 2,3.

The main limitations are the following. The simulations are planar. For the start from rest, the total angular momentum is zero, which forces highly eccentric final binaries; the mean of 0.99 in the (10, 10, 1) case is an effect of this initial condition. In the virial ensemble, 3% to 12% of the runs end with a direct ejection, with no prior pericenter, which dilutes the success rates. The definitions of most bound pair, of \(t_{\rm ej}\), and of escape attempt are our own choices. With 500 runs per row, the binomial error of the fractions is about two percentage points. Finally, as an independent check, we redid a sample of short runs with another integrator, with adaptive step size (DOP853), and with separately written code: in 16 of 17 runs the ejected body and the ejection time agreed; in the remaining, longer run the outcomes diverged, as expected for a chaotic system.

Conclusions

With three equal masses, we find no early signal of the surviving binary: it is only determined at the last close encounter. With unequal masses, the signal exists and grows with the disparity, but it is dominated by a simple rule, that the two heaviest bodies remain together. Two large masses eject the small one earlier and with fewer attempts, not more often. A natural next step is to test whether the ratio between the orbital velocity of the pair and the approach velocity of the third body predicts the outcome better than the masses.
